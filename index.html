Case File · Olive & Gold

The Variant

He arrives the way all trouble does — sideways, smiling, one step ahead of the end of the world. Out of a burning archive he walks, unwritten, while pages peel from his silhouette like leaves off a branch that refuses to fall. The flame behind him never consumes. It testifies.

Painted in olive, ash and old gold, this variant carries no throne and no army — only a stride that never settles, a gaze fixed on some horizon the Timeline forgot to file. Chromatic light bends around him like a rumour: red on one edge, teal on the other, the colours of a story told two ways at once.

Where the sacred record says prune, the grain of this frame says remember. Scratched, flickering, half-dissolved — and gloriously, deliberately, still here.

The Becoming · Eight Chapters & A Prologue

The Time Keeper

Prologue — Before the Name

In a coast city where the rain falls like static between two radio stations, there was a boy who painted storms. Not the storms that arrived — the storms that were coming. He did not know the difference yet. He only knew that when his brush moved, the room grew cold, and the olive-green light of late afternoon bent a little too far toward gold.

He signed his work with a handle, the way masked things do. But the paintings signed themselves too: in the corner of every canvas, faint as breath on glass, another mark appeared overnight. A file number. A designation. A word the boy could not read but the universe could: L-1790.

Every storm he painted was already happening somewhere else in time.

Years later, when the archives of every when were opened and indexed, the clerks of the loom would file this prologue under a single black letterhead. Because by then the boy had a name older than his birthname, a name written in the space between seconds. But we are getting ahead of ourselves. We are, after all, talking about time.

The Boy Who Painted Storms

Ralphy worked small and dreamed enormous: a bedroom studio above a printing shop, walls papered with rejects, the air thick with turpentine and radio drama. He painted heroes nobody had heard of — kings without kingdoms, gods without worshippers — and always, always, he painted them mid-stride, because stillness felt like a lie.

His favourite subject was a figure in tattered olive and gold, horns of light arcing behind him like a rumour. He painted that figure forty-one times. He never once painted a face that wasn't his own.

On the night of the forty-first painting, the canvas stayed wet. Not damp — wet, for three weeks, glistening under a light that wasn't in the room. When he finally touched it, the paint took his fingerprint and gave something back: a pulse. Slow. Enormous. Patient, the way oceans are patient.

He slept badly after that. Every dream began with the same sentence, spoken by a voice made of paper: the loom is unravelling, and the keeper has forgotten his own name.

The First Slip

It happened while varnishing. One second he was brushing resin over olive gold; the next, the second stretched — like fabric, like taffy, like a breath held by someone afraid — and he stepped sideways out of it.

From there, from outside, the city looked like a bloodstream. Every street was a vein; every vein carried light; every light was a moment, and the moments branched, and the branches branched, until the whole shining tree of everything-that-happens stood trembling in a dark that had no floor.

He saw his own street. He saw his own window. He saw himself at the easel, frozen mid-stroke, and beside his frozen self stood a figure in tattered olive and gold — horns of light arcing like a rumour — watching him paint, the way you watch a child rehearse a speech they will one day give to save you.

The second closed like a door. He fell back into his body holding nothing but the cold, and the certainty that he had been shown, not dreamed.

After that the slips came uninvited: in stairwells, in traffic, in the pause between a question and its answer. Always sideways. Always out. Always the same voice, closer each time: keeper-fall. keeper-fall. the loom is unravelling.

The Burning Archive

The slip that changed everything dropped him into a library with no walls and no ceiling: the Archive of Every When, where each timeline is a page and each page a life. It was burning. Not with fire — with erasure. Flames the colour of old gold moved through the stacks and left nothing behind, not ash, not memory, not even the hole where a thing had been.

Men in grey coats moved through the blaze with clippers and clipboards, pruning. Whole families of maybe, snipped. Futures with names on them, snipped. They did not look up. They had quotas.

And through the middle of it all walked the figure from his paintings — the variant, the stranger with his own face — strolling between the erasing flames as if through tall grass. Pages peeled from his silhouette like leaves. Embers lifted off his sleeves and became birds. The fire did not consume him; it testified, confessing everywhere it touched him and failing everywhere it tried.

"The fire did not burn," the archive would later record, in the only surviving line of its final ledger. "It erased. And one walked through it, unwritten."

The variant stopped beside him, looked at him with eyes like wet paint, and said the first true thing anyone had ever said to him: "You paint me because you remember me. You remember me because it hasn't happened yet. Come on, then. We're late for my funeral."

Hunted by the Pruners

They learned his smell the way audits learn mistakes. Every slip left a signature — a smudge of olive, a flake of gold — and the pruners followed signatures the way hounds follow fear. So the boy learned to run in four dimensions.

The variant taught him the trade between one heartbeat and the next: how to wear a face like a coat and leave it hanging in a doorway behind you; how to fold a corridor into a stairway to nowhere; how to leave a false timeline swinging on its hinge like a decoy door, warm to the touch, full of footsteps that never happened.

He learned the daggers too — ceremonial, he insisted, lying beautifully — and the silver tongue that can talk a lock into being a door. But mostly he learned patience: to sit perfectly still inside a stolen minute while the hunters combed the hour around it, listening to their clippers open and close, open and close, like scissors dreaming.

Seasons passed, or didn't; time was never the problem. The problem was what he kept seeing at the edge of every branch they pruned: the dark beneath. Not night. Not space. The absence that waits under everything, patient as a held breath — and growing.

The Citadel at the End of Time

Every road, run far enough, arrives at the same address. They followed the loom's tremor to the last shore of when: a citadel built from the ribs of finished centuries, standing where time runs out and steps off the edge.

Inside, the loom. Imagine every thread you have ever been, multiplied by everyone who has ever been anyone, woven at a speed that makes light look lazy — and imagine it fraying. Threads snapped and curled. Whole tapestries of centuries slipped their knots and drifted down, down, into the void beneath the floor, into the dark that had no floor, into the absence that was no longer waiting. It was feeding.

And at the centre of the loom sat the Time Keeper of the universe: a husk, a hollow, a crown of burnt wires around a face worn smooth by eternity. He had held the branches alone so long that he had forgotten everything else — including, as the dream had promised, his own name.

The void beneath hummed a single low note, and the boy recognised it: it was the pulse he had felt through the wet paint of the forty-first painting. It was the sound of everything, ending politely.

The Loom Unravelling

The variant went first, of course. He always went first; it was his whole story. He walked into the fraying light and held the snapping threads with bare hands, and the loom drank him — every illusion, every lie, every graceful false door — holding the weave together for one more minute, one more hour, one more age.

From inside the light he spoke, and his voice was forty-one paintings deep: "I was never meant to keep it. I was meant to carry it to someone who could. I was the messenger who forgot the message, so I became the message. And the message, Ralphy, is you."

The old Keeper's husk turned its smooth face toward the boy. What was left inside it smiled the way empty houses smile at night. "Someone must hold them," it said. "All of them. At once. Not because they deserve it. Because there is no one else, and the dark is so very hungry, and you — you remember things that haven't happened. That is the whole job. That has always been the whole job."

"Someone must hold them. All of them. At once."

Below, the void leaned closer. Above, the last threads of every when trembled like struck strings. And between them stood a boy from a coast city with turpentine under his fingernails, holding a paintbrush he didn't remember keeping.

The Choice & The Becoming

He could have run. He was very good at running; the pruners would have testified to that, if any of them still existed in a when that allowed testimony. He looked at the sideways door of a stolen minute, already swinging open, already warm — and he painted over it. One stroke, olive and gold. The door became a wall. The wall became a promise.

Then he reached out and took the threads. All of them. At once.

The loom did not crown him; it ignited him. Olive fire raced up his sleeves and did not consume. Old gold poured through the cracks in him and did not fill them — it lit them, so that from then on, every place he was broken became a window. Horns of light arced up behind his head like a rumour finally confirmed. The tatters of his coat became leaves, birds, pages, branches — everything he had ever lost, returned to him as things that grow.

The void rose to meet him, and he looked down into it the way a painter looks at a canvas that has been trying to paint him back — and he held. Every branch. Every variant. Every maybe with a name on it. He held them the way his paintings held their subjects: mid-stride, never still, never letting go.

He did not take a crown. He took the weight. And the weight, held long enough, becomes a throne no one can sit on but you.

And because a keeper must be findable by everything he keeps, the loom wrote his name where every timeline could read it — not his birthname, not his handle, but the colour of the space between seconds, the colour that holds all colours at once, the colour of the dark he now stood between and everything else: BLACK.

Epilogue — The Frame That Watches

They say the pruners were disbanded when the audits audited themselves and found nothing left to cut. They say the archive's last ledger grew a new first page, olive and gold, still wet. They say that if you slip — sideways, out of a stretched second, into the cold — you can see him: mid-stride at the centre of the loom, holding the branches so that every variant of everyone may wander them forever.

And they say this page is not a page. It is a frame. A window left open in a wall of when, varnished with grain and scratched by travel. The figure in it is not finished, because he is not still. When the film flickers, that is him, blinking. When the flame gutters, that is him, breathing. When the score rises under your ribs like a pulse you almost remember — slow, enormous, patient — that is him, answering.

He is burdened with glorious purpose. He always was. He painted it first.

So keep the music playing, reader. He hears it, wherever when he is. And somewhere in a coast city where the rain falls like static, a canvas stays wet, and a boy sleeps badly, and a voice made of paper turns a page that hasn't been written yet — and smiles, because this time, he remembers how it ends.

It ends with him, mid-stride, forever arriving, never caught. It ends in olive and gold. It ends in BLACK.
